# Learning methods for an Obsidian knowledge system

## Which learning methods justify inclusion?

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice and spacing have the strongest general support for durable learning. Explanation, diagrams, and examples help build understanding; a useful system combines these activities instead of treating note production as learning by itself.

### Cited Findings
- **Retrieval versus rereading:** Roediger and Karpicke (March 2006) compared repeated study with free recall of prose passages. Restudy helped more on an immediate test; retrieval helped more after two days or one week, despite greater confidence after restudy. This supports answering without the text before checking, rather than measuring learning by familiarity. [Original article/abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x/).
- **Breadth and limits:** Dunlosky et al. (January 2013), a rigorous review of ten techniques, rated practice testing and distributed practice high utility, self-explanation and elaborative interrogation moderate utility, and rereading/highlighting low utility. Those ratings describe generalizability and evidence, not an absolute rule that summaries or rereading are useless. [Review PDF](https://acs.ist.psu.edu/ist521/dunloskyRMNW13.pdf).
- **Applied evidence:** Agarwal, Nunes, and Blunt (14 March 2021) systematically reviewed 50 classroom experiments, encompassing 5,374 participants. Most reported medium or large retrieval benefits, across varied subjects and formats; only 6% were conducted in non-WEIRD countries. This expands support beyond laboratory word lists while limiting cultural generalization. [Systematic review](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-021-09595-9).
- **Spacing:** Cepeda et al. (November 2008) studied factual learning over intervals extending to a year. The best study gap depended on the intended retention delay; excessive as well as insufficient gaps hurt performance. This does not establish a universal interval sequence for complex notes or an optimal software scheduler. [Original article](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x).
- **Understanding:** Chi et al. (July 1994) prompted 14 eighth graders to explain a circulatory-system passage and compared them with ten students who read twice. Prompting produced greater knowledge gains; high explainers showed better understanding. Small sample and one domain limit certainty about effect magnitude. The useful activity is explaining connections or mechanisms, not simply replacing words with synonyms. [Original article](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15516709cog1803_3).
- **Transfer:** Butler (September 2010) found, in four prose-learning experiments, that repeated tests improved later responses to new inference questions, including questions in another domain, compared with repeated study. Retrieval can support transfer; this does not mean fact cards alone create programming, engineering, or design competence. [Original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20804289/).

### Inferences
- Build a trustworthy reference page first, with a concise explanation, example, limits, diagram if useful, and source. Add a small set of prompts covering its central ideas. Topic pages as navigation and prompts as practice is a practical architecture choice consistent with this evidence, not an experimentally validated Obsidian layout.
- Select what must be available mentally. Frequently needed concepts and facts merit practice; occasional lookup information can remain searchable. Original ideas, quotations, logs, and evolving plans usually need revisiting, testing, or decision-making rather than memorization. Their value is not determined by recall accuracy.
- Distinguish a summary used as a navigable reference from summary-writing used as the only study technique. Research comparing study strategies does not imply a personal knowledge base should abandon readable summaries.

### Gaps
- These sources do not compare Obsidian with other tools, establish an ideal note length or number of notes, or prove that an individual will learn more through a graph, folders, or hubs.
- Lifelong personal knowledge management includes creative ideas and changing information that typical instructional experiments do not test.

## How should useful diagrams support learning and recall?

### Takeaway
Use diagrams when relationships, sequences, spatial structure, or feedback are the thing to understand. Reconstructing their meaning from memory can combine visual explanation with retrieval; copying a polished image is a different activity.

### Cited Findings
- Karpicke and Blunt (February 2011) found retrieval superior to elaborative concept mapping for later comprehension and inference questions. In that comparison, maps were created with the material available; it does not establish that all concept mapping is inferior. [Original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/).
- Blunt and Karpicke (online 17 February 2014) compared paragraph recall and concept maps made from memory. Both retrieval formats outperformed further study on a one-week test, with no significant difference between retrieval formats. Thus a concept map can itself be retrieval practice. [Author-hosted original PDF](https://learninglab.psych.purdue.edu/downloads/2014/2014_Blunt_Karpicke_JEDP.pdf).
- Wammes, Meade, and Fernandes (September 2017) studied drawing representations of textbook definitions. Drawing improved later memory over verbatim copying, but paraphrasing produced comparable retention in their third experiment. This supports meaning-making rather than a claim that drawing always beats thoughtful writing. [Original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28756291/).

### Inferences
- For a reference diagram, choose an explicit question: “How does information flow?”, “What causes what?”, or “How do these concepts differ?” Label important relationships, not merely nodes. Match representation to content: a flow diagram for a process, comparison table for distinctions, conceptual map for relationships, or plotted curve for quantitative behavior.
- For review, hide the diagram, sketch its essential parts and connections, explain the arrows, then compare with the reference. Correct missing relationships and wrong directions; judge conceptual accuracy rather than artistic quality. Ask what changes if a parameter, component, or assumption changes. This extends the concept-map findings to a practical diagram workflow; the exact workflow has not been directly tested here.
- Visual preference can inform usability. No finding here supports assigning a fixed “visual learner” identity or claiming a matched learning style is responsible for success.

### Gaps
- The concept-map experiments used brief educational texts. Their results do not establish equivalent effects for intricate engineering schematics, mathematical derivations, or all diagram types.
- No source establishes that generated visual assets alone improve understanding without explanation, checking, or active engagement.

## What is a manageable recall workflow?

### Takeaway
Recall should be a small practice loop attached to useful notes: attempt, check, correct, and revisit later. Queue limits and prompt selection are practical sustainability decisions, not experimentally optimal numbers.

### Cited Findings
- Butler and Roediger (April 2008) found both immediate and delayed feedback improved later correct recall and reduced false answers caused by multiple-choice distractors compared with no feedback. Corrective checking matters; retrieval should not repeatedly rehearse unchecked errors. [Original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18491500/).
- Spreading practice over time has broad support, but the optimal gap depends on the retention goal and studied conditions. The evidence does not warrant prescribing one fixed sequence for every item. [Cepeda et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02209.x).

### Inferences
1. **Select:** Add only material useful to remember without searching. Keep several targeted prompts within a topic page, rather than generating a separate note for every question.
2. **Attempt before reveal:** Produce an answer aloud, in writing, through a sketch, or by solving a small example with the answer hidden. Record uncertainty before checking; “that looks familiar” is not a successful attempt.
3. **Check and correct:** Reveal a short answer with reasons, assumptions, and source. Identify exactly what was missing or wrong. If there is no understanding, return to the explanation or worked example before scheduling more guesses.
4. **Revisit later:** Use a scheduler or a simple manual due date. Successful, effortful recall can justify a longer interval; failures can justify a shorter one and repair of the prompt. These are workable heuristics, not claims of optimal intervals.
5. **Apply:** Include “Why?”, “When would this fail?”, “Compare X and Y”, “Predict what happens if…”, and new-example prompts. For EKF: explain why a Jacobian is needed, sketch predict/correct information flow, and reason about an inaccurate noise assumption. Use real problems and projects alongside review.
6. **Control the queue:** Set a time budget, limit additions when reviews accumulate, and suspend obsolete, ambiguous, trivial, or low-value prompts. A suggested short session is a usability starting point, not a research-backed duration.

Pitfalls: revealing before attempting; grading vague explanations generously; too many cards per page; memorizing isolated facts without relationships; giant multi-part prompts; confusing attractive notes with mastery; practicing erroneous AI-generated answers; and converting creative thoughts or every reference into a permanent review obligation.

### Gaps
- The selected studies do not establish an optimal daily queue size, review duration, algorithm, or scoring scale. Those choices should be evaluated by use, retention of selected concepts, and whether the workflow remains sustainable.
